## 教學目標

This EMI course introduces artificial intelligence in financial technology through a structured progression from financial data to backtesting, performance evaluation, and portfolio weighting. Students will first learn how financial data are collected, aligned, transformed, and used without information leakage. They will then construct reproducible quantitative-trading backtests that incorporate rolling or expanding estimation windows, model updating, signal updating, portfolio rebalancing, transaction costs, and realistic execution assumptions.

After the midterm assessment, students will study established portfolio-weighting methods and compare their assumptions, required inputs, risk characteristics, transaction costs, and out-of-sample stability. Each team will identify a specific limitation of an existing method, formulate a clearly defined modification, and evaluate the proposed method using common data, benchmarks, rebalancing rules, transaction costs, and out-of-sample periods.

After completing the course, students should be able to prepare and audit financial data; calculate and align returns correctly; identify look-ahead bias, information leakage, and other sources of invalid backtest results; implement fixed, rolling, expanding, and walk-forward evaluation; distinguish model retraining, signal updating, and portfolio rebalancing; incorporate transaction costs and execution assumptions; evaluate financial performance and its uncertainty; implement and compare established portfolio-weighting methods; develop a mathematically defined modification of an existing method; and communicate financial evidence, limitations, and conclusions in professional English.

## 教學內容

The course begins by establishing the relationship among financial data, trading signals, backtesting, performance evaluation, and portfolio weights. Students first learn how prices, returns, financial variables, and trading calendars are constructed. Particular attention is given to observation time, publication time, signal time, decision time, execution time, and evaluation time so that trading experiments use only information that would have been available at the relevant decision point.

Students then construct quantitative-trading backtests. The course covers signal and position rules, holding periods, rolling windows, expanding windows, walk-forward evaluation, model retraining, signal updating, rebalancing frequency, transaction costs, bid-ask spreads, slippage, turnover, and execution delay. Simple financial rules and machine-learning predictions are evaluated under the same backtesting conditions.

After constructing a valid backtest, students learn how to evaluate investment performance. The course considers returns after costs, annualized return, volatility, the Sharpe ratio, the Sortino ratio, maximum drawdown, drawdown duration, turnover, benchmark comparison, market-period sensitivity, and statistical uncertainty. Students also examine overfitting, repeated model selection, multiple testing, and data snooping. Predictive accuracy is treated as model evidence rather than as sufficient evidence of investment value.

The second half of the course focuses on portfolio weighting. Students examine equal weighting, market-capitalization weighting, weights based on normalized trading signals, inverse-volatility weighting, minimum-variance portfolios, mean-variance optimization, maximum-Sharpe portfolios, equal risk contribution, risk budgeting, hierarchical risk parity, and minimum-CVaR portfolios. Covariance estimation, estimation error, portfolio concentration, downside risk, turnover constraints, transaction-cost penalties, and robust optimization are introduced when they are relevant to the weighting methods being studied.

Throughout the second half of the course, students gradually develop their own modification of an existing weighting method. The proposed method must address an explicitly stated limitation, define its inputs and calculation procedure, satisfy portfolio constraints, specify its rebalancing rule, and be reproducible by another person. Its performance must be compared with appropriate baseline methods using the same information boundary and out-of-sample evaluation conditions.

Research articles are introduced when they help students explain an empirical or methodological problem encountered during implementation. Students are expected to distinguish what the evidence demonstrates from what remains uncertain and to limit their conclusions to the data, periods, assumptions, and tests actually examined.

## 第一週

Week 1 introduces the overall direction of the course, its learning outcomes, assessment structure, and expected student work. The instructor explains how the course progresses from financial data to backtesting, performance evaluation, and portfolio weighting. Students are introduced to the midterm quantitative-trading work, the final portfolio-weighting work, individual technical assessments, English reporting requirements, evidence-based comparison of classmates' work, reproducibility expectations, and the permitted use of generative AI. By the end of the class, students should understand what they are expected to learn, produce, explain, and defend during the semester.

## 第二週

Week 2 introduces financial prices, returns, and data frequency. Students calculate simple returns, log returns, cumulative returns, and portfolio returns and examine the consequences of using daily, weekly, or monthly data. The class discusses adjusted prices, dividends, stock splits, missing values, trading calendars, and the alignment of financial variables recorded at different frequencies.

## 第三週

Week 3 focuses on point-in-time information, financial features, and simple trading signals. Students distinguish observation time, publication time, signal time, decision time, execution time, and evaluation time. They examine how look-ahead bias and information leakage arise and learn how appropriate lags can prevent the use of unavailable information. Financial hypotheses are translated into measurable features and simple rule-based signals.

## 第四週

Week 4 introduces the construction of a basic backtest. Students convert signals into positions, trades, holdings, and portfolio returns. The class examines entry and exit rules, holding periods, position limits, cash positions, long-only and long-short strategies, and the relationship between signal timing and assumed execution prices. The purpose of the initial backtest is to establish a correct and reproducible process rather than to maximize reported performance.

## 第五週

Week 5 compares different approaches to time-series evaluation. Students examine a fixed training and testing split, rolling windows, expanding windows, and walk-forward evaluation. They investigate how window length affects the amount and relevance of available information and explain why random train-test splitting is generally inappropriate for financial time series.

## 第六週

Week 6 examines model updating, signal updating, and portfolio rebalancing. Students distinguish how often a model is retrained, how often a signal is recalculated, and how often the portfolio is rebalanced. The class compares daily, weekly, and monthly rebalancing, as well as calendar-based and threshold-based rebalancing. Machine-learning predictions may replace simple trading signals while retaining the same information and evaluation rules.

## 第七週

Week 7 introduces trading costs, execution assumptions, and benchmark strategies. Students incorporate commissions, bid-ask spreads, slippage, execution delay, and turnover into their backtests. They conduct cost-sensitivity analysis and identify the level of trading cost at which a strategy ceases to outperform its benchmark. AI-based strategies are compared with buy-and-hold, cash, simple rule-based strategies, and an equal-weighted portfolio when multiple assets are involved.

## 第八週

Week 8 focuses on performance evaluation and backtest auditing. Students evaluate annualized return, volatility, the Sharpe ratio, the Sortino ratio, maximum drawdown, drawdown duration, turnover, and returns after transaction costs. The class also considers bootstrap confidence intervals, market-period sensitivity, repeated model selection, multiple testing, and data snooping. Students audit their information timing, backtest logic, benchmarks, costs, and out-of-sample evidence before completing the midterm work. The first individual technical examination evaluates financial data, return calculation, information timing, backtesting, rolling evaluation, rebalancing, and performance interpretation.

## 第九週

Week 9 is conducted asynchronously because it coincides with the instructor's travel period and the university midterm examination week. Teams submit their reproducible quantitative-trading code, results, and English reports through the LMS. The submission must document the financial question, data, information boundary, features, trading rules, backtesting procedure, rolling or expanding windows, rebalancing assumptions, transaction costs, benchmarks, performance results, and limitations. Teams may not continue modifying the submitted strategy after the designated deadline.

## 第十週

Week 10 is devoted to individual short reports. Every student presents a concise explanation of the midterm quantitative-trading work, even when the implementation was completed as a team. Each student explains one important empirical result, one problem discovered in the data, backtest, rebalancing process, or performance evaluation, and one question about converting trading information into portfolio weights. The instructor listens to all students, identifies common strengths and problems, and uses the reports to introduce the relationship among trading signals, expected returns, risk estimates, and portfolio allocation.

## 第十一週

Week 11 introduces the transition from trading signals to portfolio weights. Students examine portfolio budget constraints, position limits, leverage, short selling, and the normalization of signals. The class compares equal weighting, market-capitalization weighting, weights based on normalized trading signals, and inverse-volatility weighting. Students begin identifying limitations that may later motivate modifications of an existing method.

## 第十二週

Week 12 introduces minimum-variance and mean-variance portfolios. Students study expected returns, covariance estimation, the efficient frontier, minimum-variance portfolios, mean-variance optimization, and maximum-Sharpe portfolios. The class examines how expected-return and covariance estimation errors can produce concentrated, unstable, or economically implausible weights.

## 第十三週

Week 13 focuses on covariance estimation, risk parity, and diversification. Students compare sample covariance matrices, shrinkage estimates, factor-based estimates, and rolling covariance estimates. They then examine equal risk contribution, risk budgeting, and hierarchical risk parity. The class distinguishes diversification of capital from diversification of risk and evaluates how different definitions affect portfolio construction.

## 第十四週

Week 14 introduces downside-risk and robust portfolio decisions. Students examine Value at Risk, Conditional Value at Risk, minimum-CVaR portfolios, and scenario-based optimization. The class considers how finite samples and extreme observations influence estimated downside risk. Regularization, portfolio constraints, turnover limits, transaction-cost penalties, and robust optimization are introduced as responses to unstable or impractical portfolio weights.

## 第十五週

Week 15 supports the development and evaluation of student weighting methods. Each team identifies a specific limitation of an existing method, such as excessive concentration, sensitivity to estimation error, high turnover, transaction costs, tail risk, unstable trading signals, or changing market conditions. The team defines its proposed modification, including its inputs, mathematical calculation or optimization model, portfolio constraints, and rebalancing rule. The second individual technical examination evaluates portfolio-weighting methods, covariance and risk estimation, portfolio constraints, downside risk, transaction costs, and the validity of empirical comparisons. All teams submit their final reports, code, and results before the first presentation session.

## 第十六週

Week 16 contains the first session of final reports. The first set of teams presents its portfolio-weighting method, financial motivation, mathematical definition, implementation, baseline comparison, out-of-sample results, and limitations. Sufficient time is reserved for questions about assumptions, data, estimation windows, rebalancing, transaction costs, portfolio concentration, and interpretation of the reported results. Teams scheduled for Week 17 may attend and ask questions but may not revise the work submitted before Week 16.

## 第十七週

Week 17 contains the second session of final reports. The remaining teams present under the same requirements and time allocation. After all reports are complete, students compare the other teams using common evidence, including out-of-sample return, volatility, downside risk, drawdown, turnover, transaction costs, portfolio concentration, and weight stability. Students do not rank their own team. Rankings received from classmates do not directly determine a team's grade.

## 第十八週

Week 18 introduces advanced issues in portfolio weighting and connects the semester's work with current research questions. Depending on the limitations observed in the final reports, the class may examine time-varying portfolio weights, market-regime changes, online portfolio selection, machine-learning approaches to portfolio allocation, distributionally robust optimization, estimation uncertainty, and transaction-cost-aware rebalancing. The instructor uses examples from student work to demonstrate how an empirical limitation can be transformed into a research question, a formal method, and an appropriate validation design.

## 成績評量

- Class participation and weekly learning evidence: 10%

- Two individual technical examinations: 40%

  - First technical examination on financial data, backtesting, rebalancing, and performance evaluation: 20%

  - Second technical examination on portfolio weighting, risk estimation, constraints, and empirical comparison: 20%

- Midterm quantitative-trading work: 25%

  - Team implementation, reproducibility, and written report: 15%

  - Individual short report and oral explanation: 5%

  - Individual evidence-based review of other approaches: 5%

- Final portfolio-weighting work: 25%

  - Team weighting method, implementation, and empirical evaluation: 15%

  - Final report presentation and oral responses: 5%

  - Individual evidence-based comparison of other teams: 5%

Students will not evaluate their own team. Rankings received from classmates will not directly determine team grades. Individual comparison scores will depend on whether the student uses common test results, financial measures, implementation evidence, and clearly stated comparison criteria.

## 指定教科書及參考書籍

The course does not require a single textbook. It uses selected English journal articles, instructor-prepared notebooks and research notes, and open or authorized financial datasets. Supporting materials cover financial data construction, backtesting, performance evaluation, covariance estimation, portfolio weighting, risk parity, CVaR, transaction costs, and robust portfolio optimization. Research readings are assigned when the corresponding empirical or methodological issue is introduced.
